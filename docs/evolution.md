# Evolution — how the design got here

This is the design history of Cealis: three architectures in three months, two mid-build reversals that mattered, and the process that ended it. Everything here describes an archived research project — never externally audited, never run in production, never holding real data; the live contracts are testnet-only and drifted behind source; the project was retired 2026-06-10. Those caveats are canonical in the repository README ("What this is not") and are not restated per-section below.

---

## V1: the guardian network, and why it was wrong

The first working system — internally "V1" or the proof-of-function build — was built in March 2026 and it did what escrow systems have always done, with better cryptography: it introduced a trusted party and then tried to constrain it.

V1 ran a Cealis-operated guardian committee. A distributed key generation ceremony produced a shared key held 3-of-5 across guardian nodes that Cealis itself deployed and operated, alongside an orchestrator API, a vault service, and a suite of nine contracts on the Base Sepolia testnet driving a six-step identity-escrow reveal flow. It was live, end to end, on rented hosting. An identity-based-encryption construction in the drand/tlock genre was explored in the same period as an alternative key-holding design.

The problem was not a bug. The problem was the shape. Cealis-operated key shares are custody: whoever runs the committee is the escrow agent, however the shares are split. Every property the system claimed — no one can open it early, no one can be compelled — reduced to "trust the operator of the guardian fleet," which is exactly the premise the project existed to remove. And the economics compounded the trust problem: backing real partner obligations with an own-operated staked custody network needs an economic-security floor in the tens of millions per partner — on the order of a $40–80M floor to absorb a $20M partner obligation — which is unbootstrappable for a solo founder pre-funding. V1 was a working demonstration of the wrong thing.

## The V2 reset: rent, don't run

In mid-April 2026 I stopped extending V1 and redesigned from the doctrine up. Two decisions define the reset.

**One system, many configurations.** V2 reframed the product from "an identity-escrow product" to a single configurable data-escrow platform in which every use case — timed release, dead-man's-switch, evidence sealing, KYC escrow — is a configuration artifact on one shared crypto-and-custody core, never a fork. The primitives catalog in docs/designs/primitives.md is the residue of that reset, including its dispositions: P12 (custodian-topology independence) deleted, P16 (auditable committee) parked, P17 (tamper-responsive custody) not carried forward — each one a V1-era assumption that the new custody design made meaningless.

**Rent, don't run.** The custody subsystem — internally "V3," designed in late April inside the V2 system — replaced the Cealis-operated committee with composition across networks other people run. In the final construction, each seal encrypts its payload once under ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439) with a fresh data-encryption key, and that key is Shamir-split (Shamir, 1979) 3-of-3 across three signing gates: Lit Protocol (G2), a rented threshold/TEE network; drand or dcipher (G3), fixed per deployment — drand attests time, tlock-style, while dcipher is the path to richer condition attestation; and a refusal-only Cealis verifier (G4), which can halt a release on narrow legal, erasure, or integrity grounds but can never open, alter, or redirect anything. The chain (G1) is the fourth gate and the mandatory objective trigger: the on-chain condition engine holds no key share, but no gate signs until it fires, so the system is a strict four-gate AND with the chain as the one place a release trigger is objective. Cealis holds no decryption-capable key material at any point.

The reset came with a process, not just an architecture. Design decisions were locked in dated, versioned records with explicit dispositions, and the whitepaper outline carried a hard-block list of anti-patterns — including any leak of V1 framing (guardian committee, master key, identity-escrow-as-identity) into V2 documents. That lock discipline is why the two reversals described below are traceable: each one cost a recorded decision, not a silent edit.

## The build: three months, spec-first, agent-orchestrated

The tracked build ran roughly three months (2026-03-13 to 2026-06-10), with May as the peak. The method was specification-first: Stage-2 specs were written before or alongside the packages that implemented them, and the whole corpus — whitepaper plus specs plus design documents — is the complete worked design behind this system. Execution was agent-orchestrated: AI agents did most of the code production under my direction, inside a staged pipeline — work-package missions in March, a rails-and-waves roadmap in May, and autonomous verify-and-close build loops in June — with verification gates between phases. The June loops closed clean, which is why the project could later be frozen safely: no half-edited state.

The build order followed a milestone ladder, M0 through M8, that worked from the cryptographic center outward: first the sealing primitives and envelope (v3-crypto — Shamir over GF(2⁸), the age-format envelope with per-stanza MACs, hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) share wrapping, the signature verifiers), then the four-gate combiner (v3-custody), then the contract stack (the condition engine, nine condition modules, and registries), then the configurator, selective-disclosure pipeline, and API around them, ending in the M8 end-to-end demo package. The field arithmetic was written constant-time — best-effort under a JavaScript runtime — and verified against locked golden vectors; the combiner and the Shamir implementation were adversarially tested, plus an environment-gated integration check that gate keys are not recoverable from the database (not part of CI). At retirement the contract suite, including fuzz and invariant tests, passed in full (re-run on 2026-07-03), and the contracts were live on the Base Sepolia testnet with their implementation contracts Sourcify-verified. That ordering is also why the state-at-retirement assessment reads the way it does — "cryptographically real, operationally unfinished": the center was nearly done, the wiring and apps were not.

## The two corrections that mattered

Two beliefs failed under adversarial review mid-build. Both are written up as defended decisions in DESIGN-RATIONALE.md and in the cryptography spec; here is each as what I believed, what broke it, and what replaced it.

**Flat Shamir.** *Believed:* a single flat threshold split of the data-encryption key across all recipient stanzas — gates and optional conditional recipients alike — was sufficient, because the threshold arithmetic itself enforces the AND. *Broke:* surplus shares held by conditional recipients could substitute for a mandatory gate's share. With enough conditional-recipient stanzas in the header, a coalition could reach the threshold without one of the gates that was supposed to be non-negotiable — the AND silently degraded toward an OR. *Replaced:* a typed hierarchical access structure in which the Lit, G3, and G4 branches are each always required — the split over the signing gates is 3-of-3, and conditional-recipient shares can never stand in for a gate branch. The construction is specified in docs/specs/cryptography-spec.md (§0.5, §6); the lesson is that a threshold number is not an access policy, and the access structure has to be typed if the policy has mandatory parties.

**HKDF over signatures.** *Believed:* the most elegant custody design derived the key directly — DEK = HKDF over the concatenated gate signatures. No shares to store, no share lifecycle at all; the signatures simply were the key. The primitives catalog still carries this era honestly: P3 and P22 in docs/designs/primitives.md describe the HKDF construction and the signature-secrecy discipline it forced, preserved as the historical record of the superseded design. *Broke:* it turned signatures into secrets. Everywhere else in cryptography, a signature is a public verification artifact; under HKDF-derivation, anyone observing all σ values could derive the key, so transport, logging, and caching of ordinary signatures all became key-material handling — that is what P22's whole secrecy discipline existed to patch — and no reveal could ever be audited without touching key material. *Replaced:* σ-as-authorization. A verified gate signature is authorization evidence that releases exactly one wrapped Shamir share to the combiner; signatures are not key material and may appear in the post-reveal audit trail, and secrecy attaches only to shares, decapsulation material, the key, and plaintext. Unwinding the HKDF design cost a protocol version bump and a coordinated back-propagation through the spec stack. It was worth it, and it is the one migration I would start from on day one next time.

The two corrections are linked: the σ-as-authorization migration reintroduced stored shares, and it was the adversarial pass over the new share structure that exposed the flat-Shamir substitution break. One fix forced the other.

## The honesty infrastructure

The claims discipline evolved the same way the cryptography did: by finding failures and building mechanisms against recurrence.

The whitepaper carried a banned-phrasings list from the start of V2 — "non-custodial" (unqualified), "decentralized," "a subpoena can't open it," "no one can block it" — because each phrase was checkably false for this design: G4 is refusal-capable, and a component that can refuse can be compelled to refuse. The list is inherited by the README and by this document.

The self-assessment discipline had to be corrected once, materially. An early internal scorecard graded the contracts 5.00/5 on the Trail-of-Bits nine-category framework; a later rewrite withdrew it to roughly 3.5/5, on the finding that the original had counted self-authored documentation as audit maturity, and instructed that the 5.00/5 never be cited. Three adversarial self-review passes (2026-05-14, 2026-06-02, and the 2026-06-03 honest-audit rewrite) produced the CRITICAL/HIGH findings set in docs/audits/; the CRITICAL fixes landed in source but were never redeployed — which is why the deployment drift disclaimer exists and is repeated wherever the live contracts are mentioned. None of this substitutes for the external audit that never happened; it is the record of what internal review did and did not catch.

## The end

The last design decision was about evidence, not cryptography. Before deciding the project's fate I pre-committed to a falsifiable, paper-only demand filter — the Fundability Fingerprint, a strict six-condition AND test a target segment had to pass cleanly — and then ran it over 157 concrete companies. The result was 0 clean fits out of 157. The failure was structural: where release triggers are genuinely objective, customers are crypto-native, small, and unpaying, and the trustless slot is held by incumbents like Safe/Zodiac and Sablier; where customers can pay, their triggers are subjective and they carry a standing legal duty to access their own data live — the exact capability conditional sealing removes. No engineering roadmap changes that correlation, and Zama-style confidential compute was already shipping the commodity adjacent primitive. Nothing in the fingerprint required working cryptography; run three months earlier, the same zero would have cost a design document instead of a codebase.

I retired the project on that evidence on 2026-06-10, closed the build loops clean, froze the tree, and produced this archive: the strongest code, the full spec corpus, the audit record including its own corrections, and this history. What remains defensible is in DESIGN-RATIONALE.md; what remains unfinished is in docs/state-at-retirement/. Conditional sealing itself — a committee-based, practical relative of witness encryption (Garg–Gentry–Sahai–Waters, 2013) — still seems to me a real primitive waiting for a buyer that exists. This repository is the record of finding out, carefully, that in 2026 that buyer did not.
